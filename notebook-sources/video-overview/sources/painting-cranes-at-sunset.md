## Painting summary

Cranes at Sunset holds a moment, not a subject — the few seconds at dusk when cranes cross the last light and the water underneath goes perfectly still. It is the studio's argument that calm can be painted, that a painting can lower a room's pulse instead of raising it.

## The stillness problem

Birds in flight are almost always painted as action: wings spread, motion blurred, the whole frame in a hurry. This brief asked for the exact opposite. The buyer wasn't looking for energy on the wall; they wanted near-suspended calm, the quiet just before the cranes set down — a piece that lowers the room's pulse, not raises it.

## The turn — one wash, never touched

The water was laid in a single wet wash and left entirely alone, so the cranes float on stillness rather than standing in it. That was the discipline: no hard edge anywhere in the piece. The early versions nearly lost it — ripples, a reflection, a catch of light, each making the scene busier. The real work wasn't adding. It was taking away.

## The last ten minutes of light

- **Amber sky** — the exact amber of the ten minutes before sunset, not a generic orange.
- **Pale rose water** — the sky's warmth, cooled and flattened into the wash as reflected calm.
- **Soft graphite cranes** — kept nearly monochrome, so they read as silhouette, not specimen.

## How it behaves in a room (the visual spine)

Painted to glow in low evening light. At midday the amber reads as plain warm light, flat and even. At six in the evening the sky and the water meet and the cranes lift off the surface. At dusk the whole piece dims to a single glowing band of light — the exact hour it depicts, and the moment it feels most alive.

## Why cranes, why dusk

In winter, when the cranes actually migrate and the evening light runs longest, the scene feels most true. In the monsoon the same well-filled fields would hide the birds entirely — so this is the dry, clear hour, the calm before the cold.

## Where it belongs

At the end of a corridor, above a bath, a west-facing wall — somewhere you pass as the day winds down and the stillness finally lands. For the stillness seeker: who values calm, who likes negative space, who would rather end the day with cranes than with a statement.
