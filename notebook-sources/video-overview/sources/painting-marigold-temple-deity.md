## Painting summary

Marigold Temple Deity is one painting in a commissioned set of three — acrylic, two by one-and-a-half feet — made to finish a home puja corner. It isn't a single icon on a wall; it's part of a corner that finally felt complete, so a family starts its day in devotion rather than in an unfinished shelf above a shrine.

## The brief

The family asked for three devotional paintings for a home puja corner — a temple deity, a Radha-Krishna, and a mother-and-child blessing — hung so the space felt finished. In their words: the corner should feel finished, the diya lit every morning, the paintings catching the light first thing. The testimonial that opened the commission: *"Our puja corner finally feels finished. The diya is lit every morning and the paintings catch the light first thing."*

## The problem — three, not one

A set of three devotional works can easily feel like three separate commissions. The challenge was to compose them as one corner — related, not repeated. The client wanted a single feeling of completion, not three pretty pictures above a shrine.

## Two requests that shaped it

- **The morning diya** — could the diya light hit the paintings first thing in the morning, because that's when the corner matters most? The work was composed so the gold catches the early flame; the corner built around the daily lamp, not the room's main light.
- **Facing the entrance** — Radha-Krishna faces the entrance so they greet anyone who comes in; the hang was reordered so the greeting deity meets you at the threshold.

## The turn — one composed corner

Three standalone icons were rejected — they looked like three commissions and the corner never cohered. One composed corner was chosen instead: related palettes and a shared gold so the three read as a single devotion. The palette carries warm gold as the unifying thread, deep marigold tying the temple deity to the welcome tradition, and soft rose keeping the blessing figures human amid the divine.

## The detail most miss

A continuous gold line runs across all three paintings so the corner reads as one devotion — the eye travels the whole corner, not three separate frames. The marigold at the deity's feet is the flower of welcome and worship, placed so the corner is dressed to receive, not just to display. The notebook reads: paint the gold to meet the diya's morning angle — the corner lives at 6am, not noon; one gold line across all three so they're a set, not a row.

## Living with it

The diya is lit every morning and the gold catches it first thing, exactly as asked. Radha-Krishna greets anyone entering the corner, as the family wanted. The mother-and-child blessing became the focal point of daily prayer.
