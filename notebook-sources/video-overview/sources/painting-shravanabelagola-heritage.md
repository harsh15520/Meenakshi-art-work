## Painting summary

Shravanabelagola · Ruins & Monolith is a two-by-three-foot acrylic commission — painted for a son, for his father. It isn't a postcard of a famous hill; it's a memory, painted small and carried into a study so the climb could continue in conversation. The monolith sits tiny among the ruins, and the light moves across it from dawn to dusk the way a remembered place lives in a room.

## The brief

The son asked for a painting of Shravanabelagola's ruins and monolith — not a postcard, but the hill as the father remembers it, to hang above his bookshelf. In his words: *"My father climbed this hill every year as a boy. This painting brings that hill back into his study."* The testimonial that opened the commission: *"My father climbed Shravanabelagola every year as a boy. This painting brings that hill back into his study — and into our conversations."*

## Memory, not monument

To this family the hill was never a tourist site — it was the father's boyhood ritual, and the son wanted to return it to him in paint. The brief asked for the monolith "small, like a memory," the opposite of the usual hero-shot. The challenge was to make one of the largest free-standing statues in the world — the monolithic Bahubali — feel intimate and remembered. A grand postcard would have missed the point; the father's relationship to the hill was quiet, not monumental.

## The composition turn

The hero composition was rejected — too postcard, the monolith dominated and the memory disappeared. The intimate composition was chosen: the monolith sits small among the ruins, as the father would have seen it climbing. The personal landmark — the flat step near the top where he always rested — was kept, named specifically in the brief.

## The palette and the climb light

The palette keeps the scene grounded: warm stone grey for the ruins (the actual granite at golden hour, not cold grey), dusty green hillside for the climb, and a pale sky left thin so the eye rests on the small monolith. The light is the visual spine. Dawn: the ruins glow briefly and the monolith is a silhouette against first light. Midday: flat and honest, the climb as the father knew it. Dusk: the stone warms and the memory feels closest.

## Living in the study

Hung above the bookshelf where the father reads, the climb is now part of the room. Family conversations about those boyhood climbs reportedly start with "look at the painting." And the small monolith reads larger the longer you live with it — as memories do.
