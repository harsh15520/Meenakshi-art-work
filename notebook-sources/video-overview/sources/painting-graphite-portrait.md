## Painting summary

Graphite Portrait proves a likeness with pressure rather than colour — a single grey field, graded by how hard the pencil bore down. It doesn't decorate a face; it holds one.

## The colour problem

A portrait in graphite is easy to misread. It risks looking like a study, not a finished piece — as if the colour simply "hasn't been added yet." The work was to make monochrome feel complete. The buyer wanted character without decoration — a face, held, nothing else.

## The turn — pressure as pigment

Tone was built from how hard the pencil pressed, not from more marks. One gradient, from the dark jawline down to the pale chin, carries the whole face. Layering strokes on top of strokes made it muddy; letting a single pressure-gradient do the work cleared everything up.

## Three grades, one hand

Graphite, 2B through 6B, on cold-press paper. The soft B grades allow the full pressure range the portrait depends on, and the tooth of the paper holds even the lightest grey without a shine. Charcoal was too smudgy for a finished wall piece; coloured pencil would defeat the monochrome point; and a digital print loses the pressure — which is the whole subject.

## Two quiet techniques

- **Lifted-pressure hatching** — strokes begin dark and rise off the paper, so the line fades without a hard stop; the edge stays alive instead of outlined.
- **Negative drawing for highlights** — the lightest areas are left as paper, never touched, which keeps them cool while the shadows warm.

## The detail most miss

The whole likeness lives in one line: trace the single gradient jaw from the dark jawline to the pale chin and the face appears. The brightest spots are bare paper never drawn — which is exactly why they stay cool where the shadows turn warm.

## Where it lives

For someone who reads a drawing as drawing — who'll lean in and see the face was made by a hand pressing, not a colour applied. Who notices craft, likes restraint, and wants a study that is also finished.
