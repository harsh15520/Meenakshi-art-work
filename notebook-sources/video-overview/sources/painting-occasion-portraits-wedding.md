## Portrait summary

The Occasion Portrait is a wedding commission — graphite on warm-toned paper, twelve by sixteen inches, made for a mother whose daughter was getting married. Some portraits capture a person; these were made to capture a day — the jewellery, the embroidery, the pinned hair of a daughter on her wedding, held longer than any photograph could.

## The brief

A mother asked for styled graphite portraits for her daughter's wedding, capturing the jewellery, the embroidery and the way her hair was pinned, so the look of that day could be kept. In her words: *"She wanted portraits that felt like the way she looked that day."* The testimonial that opened the commission: *"This was for my daughter's wedding. She wanted portraits that felt like the way she looked that day — the jewellery, the embroidery, the way her hair was pinned."*

The occasion mattered to the brief. This was a daughter's wedding — not a generic pretty face, but a specific, once-in-a-life look the mother wanted preserved exactly.

## The problem

The hard part was drawing the details, not just the face. A wedding portrait fails if the jhumkas, the embroidery and the hairpin disappear into "suggested" detail. The challenge was to render the occasion, not just the person — those details were the whole point, because they are what made it her day, not anyone's day.

## Two requests that shaped it

- **The sangeet jhumkas** — the client asked to include the jhumkas her daughter wore at the sangeet; they were added as a named detail, not a generic stud, so the portrait names the specific celebration.
- **The venue light** — the client asked whether the background could be soft and warm, like the wedding venue; the ground shifted to a warm wash so the portrait carries the feeling of the room, not a studio grey.

## The material

Graphite on warm-toned paper, with the embroidery picked out in fine white. Graphite keeps it intimate and drawn; the warm paper and the white highlights stop it reading as a cold ID sketch. Full colour was too much like a photo, pure charcoal lost the fine jewellery detail, and digital had no hand behind it.

## The 3-week build

Week 1 was reference and likeness — working from wedding photographs to lock the face and expression. Week 2 was the jewellery and embroidery — the jhumkas, hairpin and threadwork drawn in fine detail. Week 3 was the background and finishing — the warm wash added, and the portrait delivered before the memories faded.

From the notebook: on Day 4, *"Draw the jhumka first — it sets the whole festive read."* On Day 12, *"Warm wash, not white ground, or it looks like a passport photo."*

## Details people almost miss

- **The named jhumkas** — the sangeet earrings are drawn specifically, not as generic studs; that is the client's real request, preserved.
- **The embroidery thread** — the blouse threadwork is followed stitch by stitch; up close it is clearly hand-drawn, not printed.

## Visual cues for the video

- Open on the warm ground wash filling the frame, then the likeness resolving out of it — warmth first, face second.
- Make the **gold jhumka the visual star**: come in close on `#c9a227` catching the light, drawn first because it sets the whole festive read.
- Slow macro pass along the embroidery thread, stitch by stitch, so the hand behind it is unmistakable — then pull back to show it as bridal threadwork in `#b86b7a`.
- Contrast beat: a cold white/studio-grey ground versus the warm `#f3e6d2` paper — the difference between a passport photo and a portrait of a day.
- Let the single emerald bangle `#2f6b4f` land as the one cool note that makes all the warmth read as celebration.
- Time-lapse of the three weeks compressed: likeness → jewellery and embroidery → warm wash and finishing.
- Closing hero shot of the finished portrait, with the studio line: *every painting eventually leaves the studio; the story it carries never does.*
