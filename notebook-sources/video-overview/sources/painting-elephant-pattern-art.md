## Painting summary

Elephant · Pattern Art is not a decorated animal — it is a procession. Look at it for more than a moment and it stops reading as "an elephant with a pattern on it" and starts reading as a crowd walking: crimson banding across the head and back, olive settling into the legs, a sunburst blazing behind the raised trunk, looping floral medallion arcs framing the whole scene left and right. It was built to be read slowly, trunk to tail, the way you'd follow a crowd passing you on a street.

## The flatness problem

Cover a whole animal in pattern and the real risk is wallpaper — pretty, busy, but you forget there's an elephant underneath. The brief refused that trade: the buyer wanted ornament and animal at once, neither losing to the other. The studio's answer wasn't to tone the pattern down. It was to give the pattern a direction to move in.

## The turn — three passes, not one layer

The painting was built in three passes, and the order is the whole idea:

1. **The road is laid.** A cobalt-blue field goes down first — not a background, a night road. Cool and quiet, broken only by looping green medallion arcs.
2. **A current is drawn.** Before a single cell is filled, a slow line is pulled from trunk to tail, bending with the body. Not a motif — the direction the whole painting will obey. This is where the elephant begins to lean forward.
3. **The crowd arrives.** Only now do the swirls, ovals and banded cells come in — crimson across the head and back, olive settling into the legs — each hand-set at its own angle, every one following that current. The beast fills with a procession and starts to walk.

A straight, repeating grid would have flattened the animal into a tile. Bending the grid to the trunk's curl and the haunch's round is what keeps the elephant readable underneath — and what makes the pattern look like it's moving rather than printed.

## The real palette

- **Crimson red** — the ceremonial body: head, ears, back and raised trunk in dense hand-drawn banding, the colour that reads first from across a room.
- **Cobalt blue ground** — the night the procession crosses; a deep, cool field kept from overwhelming the eye by looping green medallion arcs.
- **Sunburst gold-orange** — one blazing wheel of light behind the raised trunk, the single hottest note in the painting, marking where the eye lands first.
- **Olive green** — grounds the legs; the body's crimson gives way to a quieter olive at the base, keeping the animal tethered to the earth rather than floating in ornament.

## One cell at a time

Every swirl, oval and band is placed by hand at a slightly different angle — uniform repetition would read as printed; the variation is what reads as a crowd of small, made things, not a stencil.

## The details most people miss

- **The sunburst** behind the raised trunk — a radiating wheel of gold and orange, the one blazing point, where the procession is heading, not where it's been.
- **The medallion arcs**, left and right — looping, stained-glass-like floral rosettes that frame the elephant like gates the procession passes through.
- **The colour shift** from crimson to olive — the crowned figure at the head giving way to the grounded animal at the legs.
- **The trunk's single spiral** — the one place in the whole painting where the banding breaks: the raised trunk's tip curls back on itself in a loop, the breath before the step.

## Where it belongs

Somewhere people pass and linger, not just sit and stare at — a stair landing, a long corridor walked twice a day, a reading nook that wants colour instead of calm. Painted for the patient viewer who reads slowly and would rather a wall throw a small festival than keep a polite silence — who sees a crowd before they see an animal.
