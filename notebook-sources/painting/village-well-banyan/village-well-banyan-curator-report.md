## The story

Some paintings are about a place. This one is about a routine — the walk to the well, the wait in the banyan's shade, the same task done by the same hands for generations.

Village scenes slide easily into postcards: bright, flat, that cheerful 'colourful India.' The danger was making something pretty instead of something remembered. The person who asked for this grew up at such a well — what they wanted wasn't a brochure, it was the weight of the place.

## The technique

The turn was in the tree. A banyan's aerial roots are the hardest thing to paint honestly; as a solid green mass they look like a cartoon. They were painted with a nearly-empty brush, hundreds of separate dry strokes, each root laid down one at a time. From far the tree reads solid and whole; step close and you see it is only threads holding together — how real banyan roots actually hang.

The colour stayed earth, not rainbow. Terracotta is the colour of the actual village soil, the ground and the pots. The banyan is a dusty olive, a muted green that lets the tree feel old rather than decorative. The sky is barely there — a pale wash left thin so the eye can rest.

## The detail most people miss

It is the only painting here where the stroke is the subject. Step close to the roots and the solid tree dissolves into hundreds of individual dry strokes; from across the room it is whole again. And the second pot from the left keeps its real chip — left uncorrected on purpose, because a memory isn't tidy and a perfect pot would lie about the place.

## Living with it

In a dining room it becomes a conversation piece: visitors from villages recognise their own well the moment they see it. The earth tones sit with wood and terracotta better than any bright piece would. One owner said it "smells like childhood" — meaning the colour temperature, not literally. It belongs in a home far from the village it depicts: a small, daily reminder hung where the morning light is soft — a dining room, a stair landing, a study.
