## The story

Some commissions are a single likeness. This one was five — the parents, the in-laws, and the grandmother who raised them all, drawn so a hallway could hold the people a family comes from.

The daughter's request set the brief: five graphite portraits from family photographs — the parents, the husband's parents, and the grandmother who raised her — to hang together in a hallway. In her own words, she wanted to pass them every morning and remember who she comes from. And the testimonial that opened the commission, verbatim: *"These five are my parents and my husband's parents and the grandmother who raised us all. Every morning I pass them and remember who I come from."*

The set wasn't decorative — it was a way to keep the grandmother who raised her, and both sets of parents, present in daily life. Grandmother, parents, and the couple who commissioned them, all on one wall.

## The technique

The hard part was the temperature. Five photographs from different decades, lighting and quality all over the place. The risk was five portraits that looked like five different artists. They had to read as one family, hung as one wall, not a collage of strangers.

Two decisions held the set together. First, the reference: the Diwali photographs won — everyone looked happiest and most themselves in those. The formal studio shots were rejected, too stiff, reading as ID photos rather than people. Second, the light and material: soft graphite was the one material across all five, which keeps the wall unified. The highlights were left as bare paper — kept cool and unmarked so the set feels lit by the home, not the studio. A single warm mat board ties the five different faces into one wall.

The work was built over six weeks. Day 2 of the notebook: draw the grandmother first — she's the anchor the others hang around. Day 9: kept every face soft, like the client's living-room light, not a sharp studio.

## The detail most people miss

The likeness across the set is carried by pressure, not by matching each face to its own photo. All five were drawn with the same hand-pressure range, so they read as one set from across the hall. And the grandmother's precedence — she was drawn first and centred in the planning, even though she hangs among equals. That is the quiet hierarchy of the wall.

## Living with it

What was a plain hallway is now a meeting. The set hangs at eye level so the family is met, not displayed. Visitors instinctively name the faces — it reads as a family, not a gallery. The grandmother's portrait became the one guests ask about first.
