## The story

Some loves are painted as fire. This one is painted as dusk — blue settling, gold answering, two figures who don't need to face each other to be together.

The reference almost set the whole temperature. Three were studied. A moonlit version was cool and beautiful but lost the earthly warmth the buyer wanted. A theatre duet was lively and loose but too loose for the centre wall. The chosen midday study is a warm blue that stays luminous rather than dark — keep the night warm so devotion can still be felt in it.

## The technique

The real turn came at the easel, and it is a rule still kept: gold first, blue second, or the halo dies. The warm gold halo is laid first, then the luminous blue is floated wet-on-wet over its edge so the two breathe into each other. The blue is mixed to glow, not to recede — the hardest pigment to keep alive on a dark field — and laying the gold first is what lets it live. A touch of rose on Radha keeps her human amid the divine blue; on the fifth pass her rose was pulled warmer because she had started reading as another deity, not a woman.

## The detail most people miss

It lives in the silence. The flute lies at the side, resting, not raised — the music is implied, the moment is the pause between notes. And their hands: the fingers almost meet but don't. That near-touch is the whole tension — restrained devotion rather than union. Stand close and you feel it more than you read it.

## Living with it

This is a painting for daily life, not display. Imagine it above a console where the evening lamp is lit before guests arrive, the gold catching them at the door — suited to a pooja-room antechamber, a dining console, or a stair landing. It is for someone who doesn't need the figures named: who feels the blue-and-gold temperature and already knows the love it points to, who likes warmth over spectacle and wants a daily, not a display.
