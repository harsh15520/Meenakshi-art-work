## The story

Some commissions are about a thing. This one was about an entrance — the first object every guest meets, built to say "welcome" the way a family actually means it.

The family's request set the brief: a carved wooden gate for a private courtyard entrance, hand-painted with peacocks beneath a flowering tree — something that would greet guests before they ever stepped inside. It had to say welcome the way this family actually welcomes. In the client's own words: *"It was meant to say 'welcome' the way our family actually welcomes — with live birdsong and flowers."* And the testimonial that opened the commission: *"This gate is the first thing guests see. It was meant to say 'welcome' the way our family actually welcomes — with live birdsong and flowers."*

The peacocks were not generic. They had to echo the ones on the couple's wedding invitation — a private joke and a love story carved into the front of the house. As the piece puts it: *A marriage, remembered at the threshold.*

## The technique

The hard part was the meeting of two crafts. A carved gate and a painted gate are two different things, and the challenge was to let the carving do the structure and the paint do the life, without one fighting the other. The clients wanted it to read as one object made by one hand — not a carved frame with a picture stuck on.

The material answered that: carved seasoned wood, sealed, with layered acrylic on the relief. Wood gives the gate its weight and shadow; acrylic holds the bright peacock colour without cracking on an outdoor-facing surface. The alternatives were considered and set aside — pure relief carving (lost the colour story), a printed panel (defeated the handmade point), metal inlay (too cold for a home entrance).

The gate was built over five weeks. Week 1 carved the arch and the neem trunk — the wood was shaped and the tree's form roughed in before any colour. Weeks 2–3 carved each peacock in relief, then underpainted it so the feather relief caught light. Week 4 filled in the flowering tree and sealed the whole gate against weather. Week 5 brought installation in Saharanpur, fitted to the courtyard entrance and checked in the morning light.

## The detail most people miss

Two things. First, the relief peacock eyes are carved a few millimetres proud of the surface so they catch sunlight — most visitors only notice the shimmer, not the reason it shimmers. Second, the neem is the frame, not decoration: its branches form the arch's outline. Step back and the gate is the tree. The studio note from week one said it first — make the neem the frame, not a backdrop — and the week three note followed: peacock eyes in slight relief so they catch sun; flat paint alone looked printed.

## Living with it

What was a courtyard entrance is now a greeting. Guests pause at the gate before knocking — the client says people photograph it before they photograph the house. The neem's carved leaves read differently through the day as the sun moves across the relief. It has become the family's default "we're home" marker at the end of every evening.
