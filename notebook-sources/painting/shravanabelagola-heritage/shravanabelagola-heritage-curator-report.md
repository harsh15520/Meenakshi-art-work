## The story

Some commissions are of a place. This one was of a memory — a hill an old man climbed every year as a boy, painted small and carried into his study so the climb could continue in conversation.

The son's request set the brief: a painting of Shravanabelagola's ruins and monolith — not a postcard, but the hill as the father remembers it, to hang above his bookshelf. In the client's own words: *"My father climbed this hill every year as a boy. This painting brings that hill back into his study."* And the testimonial that opened the commission: *"My father climbed Shravanabelagola every year as a boy. This painting brings that hill back into his study — and into our conversations."*

To this family the hill was never a tourist site — it was the father's boyhood ritual, and the son simply wanted to return it to him in paint. A father's memory, carried by his son.

## The technique

The hard part was the size of the monolith. The brief asked for it "small, like a memory" — the opposite of the usual hero-shot. The challenge was to make one of the most famous landmarks in the country feel intimate and remembered. A grand postcard would have missed the point; the father's relationship to the hill was quiet, not monumental.

The cultural reference sharpened the problem. The monolithic statue of Bahubali is one of the largest free-standing statues in the world — but the client wanted it as the small, familiar thing his father climbed toward. The more personal landmark was the step where he rested, which the brief named specifically as the part the father talks about.

Two compositions were tried. The hero composition was rejected — too postcard, the monolith dominated and the memory disappeared. The intimate composition was chosen: the monolith sits small among the ruins, as the father would have seen it climbing.

The palette was built to keep the scene grounded. Warm stone grey for the ruins — the actual colour of the granite at golden hour, not a cold grey. Dusty green hillside for the climb, keeping the scene a real place rather than a backdrop. Pale sky, left thin so the eye rests on the small monolith.

The painting was lit the way the hill is lived. Dawn: the ruins glow briefly and the monolith is a silhouette against first light. Midday: flat and honest, the climb as the father would have known it. Dusk: the stone warms and the memory feels closest.

## The detail most people miss

The monolith is small on purpose. It was painted intentionally small because the whole brief was "like a memory he's carrying," not a landmark — stand too far and you might read it as a faint ruin rather than the subject. And the resting step — the flat step near the top — is included because the father always rested there: a personal landmark among the famous ones, named specifically in the brief. Walk past quickly and it is just a step; slow down and it is the heart of the climb.

## Living with it

What hung above a bookshelf is now part of the room. The father reads beneath it and the climb has become part of the study. Family conversations about those boyhood climbs reportedly start with "look at the painting." And the small monolith reads larger the longer you live with it — as memories do.
