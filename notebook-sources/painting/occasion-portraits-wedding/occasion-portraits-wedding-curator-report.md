## The story

Some portraits capture a person. These were made to capture a day — the jewellery, the embroidery, the pinned hair of a daughter on her wedding, held longer than any photograph could.

The commission came from a mother. The request was for styled graphite portraits for a daughter's wedding — capturing the jewellery, the embroidery and the way her hair was pinned, so the look of that day could be kept. In the client's own words: *"She wanted portraits that felt like the way she looked that day."* And the testimonial that opened the commission: *"This was for my daughter's wedding. She wanted portraits that felt like the way she looked that day — the jewellery, the embroidery, the way her hair was pinned."*

The occasion was a daughter's wedding, and that mattered to the brief. This was not a generic pretty face — it was a specific, once-in-a-life look the mother wanted preserved exactly.

## The technique

The hard part was drawing the details, not just the face. A wedding portrait fails if the jhumkas, the embroidery and the hairpin disappear into "suggested" detail. The challenge was to render the occasion, not just the person — because those details were the whole point. They are what made it her day, not anyone's day.

Two client requests shaped the work. First, the sangeet jhumkas: the client asked to include the jhumkas her daughter wore at the sangeet, so the earrings were added as a named detail, not a generic stud — the portrait now names the specific celebration. Second, the venue light: the client asked whether the background could be soft and warm, like the wedding venue, which shifted the ground to a warm wash so the portrait carries the feeling of the room, not a studio grey.

The material was graphite on warm-toned paper, with the embroidery picked out in fine white. Graphite keeps it intimate and drawn; the warm paper and white highlights stop it reading as a cold ID sketch. Three alternatives were considered and set aside: full colour (too much like a photo), pure charcoal (lost the fine jewellery detail), and digital (no hand behind it).

The portrait was built over three weeks. Week 1 was reference and likeness — working from wedding photographs to lock the face and expression. Week 2 was jewellery and embroidery — the jhumkas, hairpin and threadwork drawn in fine detail. Week 3 was background and finishing — the warm wash added, and the portrait delivered before the memories faded.

## The detail most people miss

Two details carry the whole occasion. The named jhumkas: the sangeet earrings are drawn specifically, not as generic studs — that is the client's real request, preserved. And the embroidery thread: the blouse threadwork is followed stitch by stitch, so up close it is clearly hand-drawn, not printed.

The notebook shows the order this depended on. On Day 4: *"Draw the jhumka first — it sets the whole festive read."* On Day 12: *"Warm wash, not white ground, or it looks like a passport photo."* The festive detail came first, and the warm ground kept it from turning clinical.

## Living with it

The occasion is the point of this piece. A daughter's wedding happens once, and the look of that day — the jewellery, the embroidery, the pinned hair — was what the mother asked to have kept. Delivered inside three weeks, before the memories faded, the portrait holds that specific, once-in-a-life look exactly as it was, longer than a photograph could.
