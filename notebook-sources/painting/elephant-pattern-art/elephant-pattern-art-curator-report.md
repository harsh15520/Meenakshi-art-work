## The story

Some paintings put an animal in a scene. This one puts a scene inside the animal. Look at it for more than a moment and it stops reading as a decorated elephant and starts reading as a procession: crimson banding along the flank, olive settling into the legs, a sunburst blazing behind the raised trunk, looping medallion arcs framing the whole scene left and right, the beast leaning forward as if mid-step. It was built to be read slowly, trunk to tail, like following a crowd.

Cover a whole animal in pattern and there's a real danger — wallpaper. Pretty, busy, pleasing, but you forget there's an elephant underneath. The buyer didn't want a choice between ornament and animal; they wanted both, neither losing out to the other.

## The technique

The answer was to build the painting in three passes, in order. First, the road: a cobalt-blue field goes down, not a background but a night road — cool and quiet, broken only by looping green medallion arcs. Second, a current: before a single cell is filled, a slow line is drawn from trunk to tail, bending with the body. It isn't a motif, it's the direction the whole painting will obey — this is where the elephant starts to lean forward. Third, the crowd arrives: only now do the swirls, ovals and banded cells come in — crimson across the head and back, olive settling into the legs — each set by hand at its own angle, every one following that current.

A straight, repeating grid would have flattened the creature into a tile. Letting the grid bend with the trunk's curl and the haunch's round is what brings the elephant back — and what makes the ornament look like it's moving rather than printed.

The colour does the same quiet work: crimson red is the ceremonial body, covering the head, ears, back and raised trunk; cobalt blue is the ground it crosses, kept cool so the hot body doesn't overwhelm the canvas; a sunburst of gold and orange blazes behind the raised trunk, the single hottest note in the painting; olive green grounds the legs, so the animal stays tethered to the earth rather than floating in ornament.

## The detail most people miss

It's the trunk's spiral — the one place the pattern breaks. The raised trunk's tip curls back on itself in a single loop, a breath in the procession where the banding pauses. The sunburst behind the trunk isn't just background — it's the one blazing point the whole procession is moving toward. And the medallion arcs framing the elephant left and right aren't filler — they're the gates the procession passes through. Step into the detail explorer and the sunburst, the medallion arcs, the spiral, and the bending grid all reveal themselves.

## Living with it

This one is for someone who loves pattern and has the patience to be rewarded by a long look — who'll trace the swirls and medallions and find the elephant living inside them, who wants a festival on the wall rather than a picture. Somewhere people pass and linger works best: a stair landing, a long corridor walked twice a day, a reading nook that wants colour, not calm.
