## The story

Some portraits prove a likeness with colour. This one proves it with pressure — a single grey field, graded by how hard the pencil bore down.

A portrait in graphite is easy to misread. It risks looking like a study, not a finished piece — as if the colour simply "hasn't been added yet." The work was to make monochrome feel complete, whole, arrived. The buyer wasn't after a decorated face; they wanted character without decoration — a face, held, nothing else.

## The technique

The turn was to stop thinking of tone as something you add and start thinking of it as something you press. Instead of building the likeness with more and more marks, the whole face was carried by how hard the pencil pressed — one gradient, from the dark jawline down to the pale chin, holds the entire person. Layering strokes on top of strokes only made it muddy; letting a single pressure-gradient do the work cleared everything up.

The materials served that idea: graphite, 2B through 6B, on cold-press paper. The soft B grades allow the full pressure range the portrait depends on, and the tooth of the paper holds even the lightest grey without a shine. Charcoal was too smudgy for a finished wall piece; coloured pencil would have defeated the monochrome point entirely; and a digital print loses the pressure — which is the whole subject.

Two techniques do the quiet work. Lifted-pressure hatching: strokes begin dark and rise off the paper, so the line fades without a hard stop — the edge stays alive instead of outlined. And negative drawing for the highlights: the lightest areas are left as paper, never touched, which keeps them cool while the shadows warm.

## The detail most people miss

The whole likeness lives in one line. Trace the single gradient jaw with your eye — from the dark jawline to the pale chin — and the face appears. And the brightest spots are bare paper that was never drawn at all; that is exactly why they stay cool where the shadows turn warm.

## Living with it

This piece is for someone who reads a drawing as drawing — who'll lean in and see the face was made by a hand pressing, not a colour applied. They notice craft, like restraint, and want a study that is also finished.
