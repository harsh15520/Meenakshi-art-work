# Graphite Portrait — Infographic Spec · "A Face in Pencil Only"

> **Source type:** Gallery painting infographic (one-page cheat-sheet) · **Slug:** `graphite-portrait`

## One-line concept

A single-page visual cheat-sheet for "how a face becomes pressure" — escape the study look, the pressure-as-pigment turn, the 2B–6B cold-press material, and the two quiet techniques.

## Sections to lay out

### 1. The move in one line
- Prove a likeness with colour → prove it with **pressure**: a single grey field, graded by how hard the pencil bore down.

### 2. The colour problem (what it avoids)
- Graphite portrait cliché = reads like a study, not finished; as if colour "hasn't been added yet."
- This brief refuses that: monochrome made to feel complete.
- Why: the buyer wanted character without decoration — a face, held, nothing else.

### 3. Pressure as pigment (the key move)
- Tone built from how hard the pencil pressed, not from more marks.
- One gradient from the dark jawline to the pale chin carries the whole face.
- Layering made it muddy until a single pressure-gradient cleared it.

### 4. Three grades, one hand (material block)
- **Graphite 2B–6B** on **cold-press paper**.
- Soft B grades give the full pressure range; paper tooth holds the lightest grey without shine.
- Rejected: charcoal (too smudgy), coloured pencil (defeats monochrome), digital (loses the pressure).

### 5. Two quiet techniques (technique strip)
- **Lifted-pressure hatching** — strokes begin dark, rise off the paper; line fades without a hard stop (edge stays alive, not outlined).
- **Negative drawing** — lightest areas left as bare paper, never touched (stays cool while shadows warm).

### 6. The detail most miss
- The whole likeness = one dark-to-pale jawline. Trace it and the face appears.
- Brightest spots = bare paper never drawn — why they stay cool.

## Visual cues
- Hero crop: the single pressure-gradient jaw resolving into the face (`/images/custom/oil-painting-7.webp`).
- A pressure strip: dark jawline fading to pale chin, with a "pressure" arrow showing hard-to-soft press.
- Two small diagrams: a hatched stroke lifting off the paper; a highlight left as bare paper (cool) against warm shadow.
- Keep it calm and monochrome — the infographic should *look* like the drawing.
