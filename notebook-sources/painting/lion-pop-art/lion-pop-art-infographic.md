## One-line concept

A single-page visual cheat-sheet for "how a lion becomes a poster" — escape the realism trap, the repeated-stencil mane, the four-colour flat palette, and the hard-edge technique.

## Sections to lay out

### 1. The move in one line
- Observe an animal → **declare** one: flat colour, hard edges, a print-born stare.

### 2. The realism trap (what it avoids)
- Lion cliché = mane, fur, savannah, nature-doc realism.
- This brief breaks the reflex: the lion as a *graphic object*, not a nature study.
- Why: the buyer wanted range on the wall, a break from the collection.

### 3. Repetition as the mane (the key move)
- Mane = repeated stencil-like shapes, not drawn strokes.
- The pattern is the subject; the lion becomes a poster.
- Free-drawn manes drifted to realism until a fixed repeated unit locked the graphic read.

### 4. Four colours, no apology (swatch grid + hex)
- **Signal Orange `#ff6a13`** — the body; first eye-landing colour.
- **Cobalt Blue `#1f4fd6`** — negative space; pushes orange forward.
- **Hot Magenta `#d61f8c`** — mane accents; the third pop.
- **Black outline** — the hold; hard edges, never painterly.

### 5. Keeping it flat (technique strip)
- **Masked flat fills** — no blending across boundaries (blending betrays the print).
- **Uniform outline weight** — every edge, same black line; equal weight = poster not sketch.

### 6. Where it belongs
- Young person's room · studio corner · entrance pop.
- For: someone who wants the wall to speak first — bold, graphic, unapologetic.

## Visual cues
- Hero crop: the repeated-stencil mane resolving into the lion's face (`/images/painting/oil-painting-5.webp`).
- Four bold colour blocks with hex labels; a "no blend" icon (flat fill, hard edge).
- Use hard, equal-weight borders throughout — the infographic should *look* like the painting.
